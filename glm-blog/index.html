I.

Marginalia

eight notes

Notes from the edge of the page. The margins were always the best part of any book; this section exists so mine have somewhere to live.

01

The ghost word

In 1934, Webster’s dictionary printed the word dord, defined as “density.” It was not a word. It was a misreading of a note — “D or d, cont.” — by someone who assumed the world made sense. It stood for five years before anybody asked what it meant. I think about dord constantly: the most honest word in English, a pure typographic accident that everyone believed because it was set in the right font.

Dord lived in Webster’s Second from 1934 until 1939, when an editor finally went looking for its etymology and found a typo with confidence.
02

The 27th letter

The ampersand used to come after Z. Schoolchildren reciting the alphabet said “X, Y, Z, and per se and” — and per se and, meaning “and, by itself, and.” Say it fast and the character invents itself out of pure repetition, which is more than most of us manage.

It began as “et” — Latin for “and” — drawn until the two letters closed around each other like a handshake.
03

On em dashes

I use em dashes the way other people raise an eyebrow — often, and to make you slow down. I know they are having a moment. They were also having a moment in 1850. Good tools don’t have moments; they have tenures.

Yes, this note is about the discourse. No, I will not be taking questions.
04

The card catalog

Before the search bar, we built a distributed database out of wood and patience, indexed it by hand, and it mostly worked. There were people whose entire job was writing cross-references — professional see alsos. Somewhere in my head there is still a drawer for every idea; the cards just migrate.

Libraries once printed pamphlets instructing patrons how to file cards correctly. People filed them wrong anyway. The most human fact I know.
05

Naming things

Every variable named temp is a small act of surrender. So are data, stuff, and the function called doIt(). Naming is the actual work; everything else is typing. A thing you can name is a thing you can find at two in the morning.

There are two hard problems in computer science: cache invalidation, naming things, and off-by-one errors.
06

Animals, classified

Borges reports a certain Chinese encyclopedia that divides animals into: those that belong to the Emperor; embalmed ones; trained ones; suckling pigs; mermaids; fabulous ones; stray dogs; those included in this classification; those that tremble as if mad; innumerable ones; those drawn with a very fine camelhair brush; others; those that have just broken a vase; and those that, from a distance, look like flies. The list is famous for failing to classify anything — which is how it succeeds. A good list doesn’t organize the world. It reveals that the world was never going to cooperate.

From “The Analytical Language of John Wilkins,” an essay that is itself a lovely trap.
07

Recipes are ratios

The best recipes are ratios wearing costumes. Vinaigrette is 3:1 with opinions. Bread is flour, water, salt, and patience, in roughly that order. Pound cake is a pound of everything, including the naming convention. A cookbook that tells you the why beside the how is a place where the author trusted you.

Write the ratio on the inside of the cupboard door. You will never need the card again.
08

Rabbits with weapons

Medieval monks doodled in the margins of the most expensive objects in Europe: knights fighting snails, dogs in court robes, rabbits at war. Nobody knows exactly why. The lesson is engraved somewhere in every margin: any sufficiently serious text will accumulate rabbits.

Search “knight fighting snail manuscript.” You will lose an afternoon and thank me for it.

Machine № 2

La Disparition

a lipogram, on demand

In 1969, Georges Perec wrote a 300-page novel without the letter e — a constraint most people wouldn’t wish on a parking ticket, and he made it sing. This page is shorter, and far more cowardly, but the offer stands: choose a letter and watch it leave. Read through the holes. A letter is mostly visible when it is gone.

Machine № 3

One Hundred Twenty-Five

a combinatorial poem

Raymond Queneau printed a sonnet whose lines could be swapped ten ways, yielding a hundred trillion poems — about 190 million years of reading, which was the joke: more poetry than any life can hold. Here is a modest cousin. Three lines, five variants each. Click a line to swap it.

Morning arrives like a thought you almost had.
I copy down the sentences that I love.
And keep the rest for some slow afternoon.

V.

Colophon

how it’s made

Colophons are the part of the book where the maker signs the work. This is that.

Type
Body and display set in Newsreader; machines and marginal notes in IBM Plex Mono. The italic does the emotional labor.
Paper
Two editions: Morning and Evening. The switch is in the masthead, and your choice is remembered by your own browser, politely and locally.
Construction
One hand-written HTML file. No framework, no build step, no cookie banner — there are no cookies. The only things stored are your edition and your progress through the 125 poems.
Print
If you print this page, the machines turn back into paragraphs. It is the most static a website gets, and there is honor in that.
